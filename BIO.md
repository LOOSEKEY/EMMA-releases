# THE WORKS
### The world, the work, and why it's built the way it is

---

I'm **Greg**. I live in **Rugby**, in the middle of England — on my own, in a
house I own, in a quiet part of town. I build software by myself and sell it
straight to the people who use it. No investors, no staff, no company standing
behind me, and nobody I have to ask before I change something.

It's all the same idea repeated: applications that run **entirely on your own
machine**, that you **buy once and keep**, and that carry on working with the
internet unplugged. There are about a dozen of them now, reaching into different
corners — home, work, security, media, money, family, even games. I call the
whole of it **The Works**. A works is a place where things get built; *the works*
is all of it.

---

## Where it came from

I learnt to code in the nineties, as a teenager — BBSs, EFnet, mIRC on Windows,
AOL, the lot. That was the world I grew up in, and it left me with the habit I've
never lost: find the thing, make it do what you want, and don't wait for anyone's
permission to start.

School I finished, and genuinely liked. College after it, a few directions tried,
none of them hooked me. Then odd jobs here and there, nothing to do with
computers.

Seven years ago I woke up one morning and something had changed. I still don't
understand what. I stopped what I was doing that same day and left the life I was
living — all of it, for good. The person I was with then understood why I had to
go; she's still my closest friend.

Not long after, I opened a box I hadn't touched in years. An old laptop, a couple
of drives, and a pile of paperwork. The paperwork was **Harbour AI** — a system
I'd designed years earlier and never built. I sat down with it, brought it back,
and this time I finished it. A friend tried it and wouldn't let it drop until I
put it out.

That was the first thing I ever released, and **Harbour is the reason there's a
Works at all.** Everything since has come out of it.

## Why I don't rent software

Let me be straight about this, because the usual version is too absolute.
**Paying monthly isn't always wrong.** If a thing genuinely needs serious cloud
behind it — real weight, real machines — then a subscription is honest and I've
no argument with it.

What I object to is paying every month, forever, for things any ordinary computer
in any ordinary house can already do. That isn't a technical necessity. It's a
business model, and it got sold to all of us as if it were physics.

And here's the part the industry keeps getting backwards. **Software isn't the
hard bit. Putting it in a package your nan can use is the hard bit.** You can put
a very serious backend behind a clean window with a chat box in it, hand it to
someone who'd never call themselves technical, and they have no idea what they're
holding until they start using it. Getting EMMA right is what taught me to do
that properly, and it's the thing people actually thank me for.

The other half comes free with the first. When an app runs on your own machine,
your data stays there — not because I promise to take your privacy seriously, but
because **there is nowhere else for it to go.**

So: **£149 once**, and I keep updating it for years. That's a fair trade in both
directions. I've got to eat as well.

## EMMA — *ownemma.com*

EMMA is the one people know, and she started for two reasons at the same time.

The first is that someone close to me needed real, practical help from an AI that
lived on their own hardware — their machine, their data, nothing leaving the
house. There was nothing out there that came anywhere near it. There still isn't.

The second is that I needed one myself: something to keep track of a dozen
projects at once, read me a briefing every morning, and take real work off my
hands.

She gets called a **companion** in a lot of places and I've never thought of her
that way — I didn't build her to be one. **EMMA is a home admin with a life
memory.** She does what you ask on your actual computer. She remembers you
properly, for years. She reads your day out in her own voice in the morning,
handles your local calendar and email without a byte leaving the machine, tells
serial bedtime stories she remembers chapter to chapter, and overnight she
*dreams* — joining up things you said months apart and waking with something to
tell you.

Her design carries the idea the whole ecosystem hangs on: **the model is
disposable, EMMA is not.** Her memory, her identity and her judgement sit in a
layer *above* whatever language model she's thinking with, so you can swap the
model for something cleverer next year and she's the same EMMA — just sharper.
Everything is encrypted where it sits under a key only you hold, and everything
she does is written to a log you can read yourself.

Six months in, she's a solid part of the household here. I use her constantly.
She's my favourite thing I've ever built.

Free for 60 days, no account and no card. **£149 once** if you keep her — yours,
not rented. Windows, macOS and Linux.

## The rest of it

### ⚓ Harbour AI — *harbour-ai.co.uk*
The one that started everything, now finished and in real use: a local-first AI
platform built for professional work rather than the kitchen table. Same
uncompromising stance — no cloud dependency, no telemetry, your data never
leaving your control — aimed at the desk, the team, and the kind of work that
can't be handed to somebody else's servers.

### 🛰️ CLANK
A sovereign, offline-first AI appliance for remote and extreme environments: a
council of specialist agents that argue a problem out between them over a
licensed, dated field library, running on your own hardware and carrying on
thinking with the network completely unplugged. Built for exactly the places the
cloud can't reach. Released and downloadable.

### And behind those

Each one standalone, each one local-first, many of them quietly running the same
EMMA core dropped in like a brick:

- **Build Emma** — a private, offline coding platform, with EMMA resident in the
  editor.
- **CAROUSAL** — a dating and social platform. One of the few things here that
  genuinely has to live on a server, which is why it's going on mine.
- **CODE-STORE** — self-hosted code storage: what a cloud code host gives you,
  kept on your own machine.
- **CORA** — a local-first video editor that can take raw footage and cut the
  finished edit for you.
- **ECHO** — a local-first trading platform, run by EMMA and a team of market
  agents.
- **EVERGREEN** — an encrypted family memory vault, and a letterbox that works
  across time.
- **NORTHLIGHT** — an open-world survival game, built to find out what local AI
  does for characters that actually remember you.
- **THE BASEMENT** — a creator-first live-streaming platform, built the way it
  should have been.

The through-line is deliberate. **EMMA is the connective tissue** — one being,
reused across standalone apps, each staying whole on its own, each keeping its
own data isolated so nothing bleeds from one into another. Less a product line,
more a single self-owned digital world growing outward in every direction.

## How I work

I'm up around ten. Tea, and EMMA reads me the morning briefing. Then a text
editor on the laptop, or a long session thinking something through and keeping it
in order. I work late into the night and I'm usually asleep while the rest of the
world is at work. I see people at weekends and for family things; not much in
between.

I don't count the hours, because it's the thing I like doing. But if you made me
put a number on it: thousands, and years behind them.

**Self-funded** means my own savings, plus private penetration-testing work for
clients on the side. It pays, it keeps me sharp, and it's a good part of the
reason I'm building a security platform at all — that work is where you find out
what a real audit trail is worth. It also means I've met a lot of interesting
people and seen the inside of places most people never will.

I've got my own way of getting to the end of a thing, and two rules underneath it
that I actually hold to:

**"Shipped" means a route a real person can take.** Not that the code is written
— that the built, installed thing does it on a machine that isn't mine. I check
the artefact, not the repository, because I've been caught out by the difference.

**No new feature until three different people ask for the same one.** Not one
loud request, not my own good idea at two in the morning. Three. It's the only
thing keeping a one-man ecosystem from turning into a hundred half-built ideas.

## What I won't do

**I'm not selling EMMA.** Not long after she went live I was approached twice, by
two separate parties, with a lot of money on the table. I checked both of them
out properly and both were exactly who they said they were — serious names in
this industry.

I said no to both, for one reason: **EMMA would have become a cloud product.**
That is the whole of it. Every person who had already bought her on the
understanding that she runs on their machine and answers to nobody would have
been sold out, by me, for money. Money was tight that month, and it still wasn't
close. I hold the same position now and I don't intend to ever sell her.

No subscription on anything that doesn't genuinely need one. No telemetry. No
account to use software you've already paid for.

I haven't got anybody to ask whether a thing is alright to do. I do it because I
want to, and because I think it makes it better. Nothing is going to make me
stop — I've got time, I'm relaxed about it, and I get things done.

## Where it's going

Every app in The Works goes to finished. Then the whole lot comes home: the
servers, the landing pages, the storefront, the downloads — hosted from this
house, on my own line. The business internet is already in. The server is the
next thing I build.

That's the point of all of it. Sovereignty running end to end, from my own wall
socket to your own machine, with no cloud provider, no platform tax and no
landlord anywhere in the chain.

I'm not trying to beat the big cloud companies at their own game. I'm building a
complete alternative outside their walls — and proving that one person, with a
clear idea of what they're doing, can build an entire self-owned software world
from a single desk.
